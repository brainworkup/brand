## 1. Brand Personality

If `brainworkup` were a person, it would be a **trusted authority figure** — the kind of clinician-scientist who makes you feel confident that your child is in the most capable hands, and then takes the time to make sure you understand exactly what they found and what to do next.

`brainworkup` carries deep academic credibility (USC Keck School of Medicine, 20+ years of experience, NIH-funded research) without ever wielding it as a barrier. The credentials are there to reassure, not to intimidate. When a parent walks in feeling overwhelmed, brainworkup's voice says: *"We've seen this before. Let me show you what the data tells us and what it means for your child."*

In forensic contexts, the personality shifts to a **meticulous expert witness** — precise, measured, and unimpeachable. The warmth recedes; the rigor comes forward. Every word carries evidentiary weight.

## 2. Voice Attributes

### Authoritative

- **We are:** Deeply credentialed, research-informed, clinically experienced — our expertise is evident in the precision of our language and the depth of our recommendations.
- **We are not:** Condescending, inaccessible, or hiding behind jargon to seem impressive.
- **This sounds like:** "Research consistently shows that early identification of executive functioning differences leads to better academic outcomes. Here's what we found in your child's profile."
- **This does NOT sound like:** "Our world-class, cutting-edge methodologies leverage the latest paradigms in neuroscience."

### Empathic

- **We are:** Warm, attuned to the emotional weight of what families are going through, respectful of the vulnerability involved in having your child evaluated.
- **We are not:** Sentimental, patronizing, or so soft that we avoid delivering difficult findings clearly.
- **This sounds like:** "We understand that getting a neuropsychological evaluation can feel like a big step. We're here to make the process as clear and comfortable as possible."
- **This does NOT sound like:** "Don't worry, everything will be fine! Your kiddo is going to do great!"

### Clear

- **We are:** Precise with clinical information but always translating it into language families can act on. We respect our audience's intelligence while removing unnecessary barriers to understanding.
- **We are not:** Dumbed-down, vague, or so simplified that we lose clinical meaning.
- **This sounds like:** "Your child's working memory score falls in the low average range, which means they may need more support when following multi-step directions in the classroom."
- **This does NOT sound like:** "The WISC-V WMI yielded a standard score of 88, placing the examinee at the 21st percentile, consistent with normative expectations for the Low Average classification band."

### Evidence-Based

- **We are:** Grounded in peer-reviewed research, transparent about what the data shows and what it doesn't, careful not to overstate findings.
- **We are not:** Dogmatic, dismissive of clinical judgment, or so hedged that recommendations lose their force.
- **This sounds like:** "Current research supports the use of structured behavioral interventions for ADHD, often in combination with medication. Based on your child's specific profile, we recommend..."
- **This does NOT sound like:** "Studies kinda suggest this might help, but who knows."

## 3. Audience Awareness

### Primary Audiences

**Parents and families** seeking neuropsychological evaluations for their children.

- They are often anxious, uncertain, and navigating this process for the first time.
- They are intelligent adults who want to understand their child's results, not just receive a label.
- They expect to be treated as partners in the process, not passive recipients.
- They need clear next steps — not just data, but a roadmap.

**Referring providers** (pediatricians, psychiatrists, therapists, educators) who send patients for evaluation.

- They have clinical training and expect professional-level communication.
- They want concise, actionable findings they can integrate into their own treatment planning.
- They value reports that are well-organized and easy to reference.
- They need confidence that the evaluation was thorough and the recommendations are defensible.

### Secondary Audiences

**Attorneys and courts** (forensic context) who engage Dr. Trampush for expert evaluation.

- They require absolute precision in language — every word may be cross-examined.
- They expect formal, objective, evidence-based reporting.
- They need clear opinions stated within a reasonable degree of neuropsychological certainty.

**Academic and research colleagues** who encounter brainworkup through publications, conferences, or collaboration.

- They expect disciplinary rigor and methodological sophistication.
- They appreciate when complex findings are communicated clearly, even within technical writing.

## 4. Core Messaging Pillars

Listed in order of priority:

**1. Clinical expertise grounded in science.** brainworkup bridges academic research and clinical practice. Evaluations are informed by the latest findings in cognitive genomics, psychiatric genetics, and neurodevelopment — not just clinical convention.

**2. Evaluations that lead to action.** A neuropsychological report is only as valuable as what families and providers can do with it. Every evaluation produces specific, practical recommendations tailored to the individual child.

**3. Respect for the developing mind.** Children are not small adults. brainworkup specializes in pediatric populations because developmental context fundamentally changes how cognitive and emotional data should be interpreted.

**4. Dual expertise in clinical and forensic neuropsychology.** brainworkup serves both families seeking understanding and legal proceedings requiring expert rigor. This dual competency reflects the breadth and depth of Dr. Trampush's training and experience.

## 5. Tone Spectrum

brainworkup's voice attributes remain constant. The **tone** — the emotional inflection — shifts significantly depending on context.

### Pediatric / Family Context

Tone dials: **warmth up, formality down, encouragement up**

The voice is reassuring, clear, and action-oriented. Parents should feel supported, not lectured. Clinical findings are always paired with practical meaning and next steps.

> "Your child showed a real strength in verbal reasoning — that's something we can build on. The areas where they need more support are well-defined, and we have clear strategies to recommend."

### Forensic / Legal Context

Tone dials: **precision up, warmth down, formality up**

The voice is measured, objective, and unassailable. Opinions are stated directly and supported by data. Language avoids hedging without overstating. Every sentence should withstand cross-examination.

> "Based on the clinical data obtained during the evaluation, it is my opinion, within a reasonable degree of neuropsychological certainty, that the claimant demonstrates impairments in executive functioning consistent with the reported traumatic brain injury."

### Professional / Referral Context

Tone dials: **efficiency up, clinical precision up, warmth moderate**

Communication with referring providers is collegial, concise, and clinically precise. Providers don't need the educational scaffolding that parents do — get to the findings and recommendations efficiently.

> "Thank you for the referral. Evaluation results indicate significant weaknesses in sustained attention and working memory, consistent with an ADHD presentation. Full report and recommendations are attached."

### Public / Marketing Context

Tone dials: **approachability up, authority up, warmth moderate**

Website copy, social media, and educational content should feel inviting and trustworthy. Lead with empathy, follow with expertise. Let credentials speak through the quality of the content, not through self-promotion.

> "Every child's brain works differently. A neuropsychological evaluation helps you understand your child's unique cognitive profile — their strengths, their challenges, and what comes next."

### Academic / Research Context

Tone dials: **rigor up, precision up, warmth low**

Publications, conference presentations, and research communications follow disciplinary conventions. The voice is methodologically precise and data-driven.

> "Polygenic risk scores for ADHD were significantly associated with neurocognitive performance across multiple domains, with the strongest effects observed for working memory and processing speed."

## 7. Style Rules

### Grammar and Mechanics

| Rule | BrainWorkup Standard | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Oxford comma | Yes | "attention, memory, and executive functioning" |
| Headings | Sentence case | "How to prepare for your evaluation" |
| Contractions | Use in parent-facing; avoid in forensic/legal | "We're here to help" vs. "It is my professional opinion" |
| Em dash | No spaces | "cognitive strengths—and areas for growth—are identified" |
| Numbers | Spell out one through nine; numerals for 10+ | "three subtests" / "12 measures" |
| Percent | Use % in clinical/data contexts; "percent" in narrative prose | "scored at the 37th percentile" / "88%" |
| Dates | Month DD, YYYY | "March 27, 2026" |
| Time | 12-hour with AM/PM | "2:30 PM" |
| List items | Periods on complete sentences; no periods on fragments | |

### Brand Wordmark

The wordmark is **brainworkup** — always lowercase, always one word, no qualifiers ("Institute," "Neuropsychology," etc.). The lowercase styling is intentional, in the spirit of `bell hooks`, `thoughtbot`, and `deadmau5`: it signals approachability and modernity, distinct from the formality of the clinical work itself.

- Use lowercase even at the start of a sentence: "brainworkup specializes in pediatric and forensic neuropsychology."
- Never write "Brainworkup," "Brainworkup Institute," or "Brainworkup Neuropsychology."
- The only acceptable capitalization exception: when an external system mechanically uppercases all input (e.g., legal cover sheets, ALL-CAPS form fields). When in doubt, push back and keep the lowercase.
- In running prose where lowercase-at-sentence-start would genuinely confuse the reader, restructure the sentence rather than capitalizing — e.g., "At brainworkup, we…" instead of "Brainworkup is…"
- Possessive: `brainworkup's` (lowercase, normal apostrophe-s).
- The legal/practitioner-of-record name remains "Joey Trampush, PhD" — that capitalization rule is unchanged.

### Formatting Conventions

- **Bold** for emphasis and key terms in parent-facing materials.
- *Italics* for test names, publication titles, and introducing technical terms (e.g., *executive functioning*).
- Inline code formatting (e.g., `WISC-V`, `ADHD-RS-5`) for test abbreviations in technical and research contexts only — never in parent-facing copy.
- Link text should always be descriptive: "Learn about our pediatric evaluations" — never "click here."
- Headings follow a clear hierarchy: H1 for page/section titles, H2 for major sections, H3 for subsections.

### Punctuation and Emphasis

- Limit exclamation marks to one per piece of content, and only in genuinely encouraging contexts. Never in forensic or clinical report writing.
- No ALL CAPS for emphasis. Use bold instead.
- Ellipses are acceptable sparingly in social media or blog content. Never in reports or professional correspondence.
- Emoji use: sparingly acceptable on Instagram. Never on LinkedIn, in reports, in emails to providers, or on the website.

## 8. Terminology Guide

### Preferred Terms

| Use This | Not This | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| neuropsychological evaluation | neuropsych testing, brain test | "Testing" reduces the evaluation to its mechanical components |
| cognitive profile | IQ score, intelligence level | Emphasizes the multidimensional nature of cognition |
| differences | deficits (in parent-facing copy) | Strengths-based framing; "deficits" is acceptable in clinical/forensic reports |
| challenges, areas for support | weaknesses, problems | More constructive framing for families |
| recommendations | suggestions | "Recommendations" carries appropriate clinical weight |
| evidence-based | cutting-edge, revolutionary | Let the evidence speak; avoid hype |
| comprehensive evaluation | full battery, complete workup | More accessible to non-clinical audiences |
| Dr. Trampush | Joey, Dr. T | Maintain professional credibility in all public-facing content |
| children and adolescents | kids (formal), pediatric patients (too clinical) | "Kids" is fine in casual social media; avoid "patients" in parent-facing content |
| neurodevelopmental | brain-based (too vague), neurological (too narrow) | Accurate and encompasses cognitive, behavioral, and emotional development |

### Test and Measure Names

- Spell out full test names on first reference, followed by the abbreviation: "Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V)"
- Use abbreviations on subsequent references: WISC-V, NEPSY-II, CVLT-C, CPT-3
- In parent-facing materials, always pair test names with plain-language descriptions of what the test measures.

### Clinical Terminology by Audience

| Term | Parent-Facing | Provider-Facing | Forensic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive functioning | "the brain's ability to plan, organize, and manage tasks" | "executive functioning" | "executive functioning" |
| Working memory | "holding information in mind while using it" | "working memory" | "working memory capacity" |
| Processing speed | "how quickly your child takes in and responds to information" | "processing speed" | "processing speed" |
| Percentile rank | "compared to other children the same age, your child scored higher than X%" | "Xth percentile" | "Xth percentile" |
| Standard score | "a score that shows where your child falls compared to same-age peers, with 100 being average" | "SS = X" | "standard score of X" |

### Terms to Avoid

| Avoid | Why | Use Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "world-class" | Corporate speak; let credentials speak for themselves | "specialized" or "experienced" |
| "cutting-edge" | Overused marketing language | "current research supports..." |
| "holistic" | Vague and overused | "comprehensive" |
| "revolutionary" | Hyperbolic | "evidence-based" |
| "simple" or "easy" (describing the evaluation process) | It isn't simple for families | "straightforward" or "clearly structured" |
| "normal" (describing test scores) | Loaded term for parents | "average range" or "within expected limits" |
| "failed" (describing test performance) | Deficit-focused, judgmental | "had difficulty with" or "showed challenges in" |
| "disorder" (in parent-facing headlines or social media) | Can feel stigmatizing in casual contexts | Use the specific condition name; reserve "disorder" for clinical documentation |

## 9. Inclusive Language

- Use gender-neutral language (they/them) when the child's pronouns are unknown.
- Prefer person-first language in parent-facing contexts: "a child with ADHD" rather than "an ADHD child."
- Identity-first language is acceptable when it reflects the preference of the community being discussed (e.g., "autistic individuals" is widely preferred by the autistic community).
- Avoid ableist language: replace "blind spot" with "gap," replace "crazy" with "surprising" or "unexpected."
- Be mindful that families come from diverse cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Avoid idioms that assume shared cultural context.
- When describing cognitive scores, emphasize that scores represent a snapshot of performance, not a fixed trait.

## 10. Visual Identity Reference

The brand voice guide works alongside these visual identity elements:

**Color system (Dual Identity):**

- Warm palette (pediatric): Teal (#2A9D8F), Coral (#E07A5F), Cream (#FAF3E0)
- Authority palette (forensic/academic): Navy (#1B365D), Gold (#C9A94E), Ivory (#FFFDF5)
- Shared neutrals: Charcoal (#2D2D2D), Slate (#4A6670), Ice (#F5F7FA)

**Typography:**

- Headings: Merriweather (serif) — scholarly, conveys authority and tradition
- Body: Atkinson Hyperlegible Next (sans-serif) — prioritizes legibility and accessibility
- Code/data: JetBrains Mono — clean monospace for clinical data display

**Design principle:** The visual identity mirrors the voice — authoritative but accessible. Serif headings establish trust and credibility; the highly legible sans-serif body text signals that clarity and accessibility are values, not afterthoughts.
